A game about rooms, and who they are built for.

Built on monotropism, the account of autistic attention put forward by Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser and Wenn Lawson. One rule runs the whole simulation: comfort does not average. One bad spot on the route is the visit.

Commissions

Owners with rooms, rooms with problems. Finish one and word gets round. Your budget on each job is the owner’s goodwill — spend it well.

Rooms you have finished

Nothing to do in these. No timer, no visitor to get through, nothing to fail. Just somewhere quiet that is quiet because you made it so.

Three people, one morning, one room.

Mara’s problem was never Ollie’s, and Ollie’s was never Jun’s, and the room never asked any of them to be different — because you changed the room instead. That is the whole argument of this game, and it is not a special measure. It is just what design is for.

This game will not shorten a waiting list. It is an argument you can hold: attention that runs deep is not a fault to train out, and the cost of a bad room lands on whoever is quietest about it. If a doorway, a speaker, or a bank of fluorescent tube looks different to you now — it worked.

How to play

  1. Read the brief. Who is coming, and what the owner will not bend on.
  2. Survey the room. The floor shows the load; the list on the right splits it into channels — noise, flicker, glare, crowding, retreat. Hover anywhere to read what is worst at that spot.
  3. Change the room. Drag anything that moves. Buy from the tray and click where it goes. Delete removes a bought item and refunds it. The budget is the owner’s goodwill.
  4. Watch them walk it. Look shows the visit from above; Walk it puts you at their eye height, on the exact route they took. Interruptions land where they landed. The bars show reserve and absorption as they go.
  5. Everyone must make it, and the owner’s rules must hold. One person comfortable is not enough, and comfort does not average: one bad spot on the route is the visit.
  6. Stuck? Press Where do I start? in Plan. It names who is stopped and what by, and suggests the single biggest improvement — or places it for you if you would rather just see it work.
  7. Walk yourself lets you move through the room with WASD. There is deliberately nothing to cope with: no ear defenders, no push-through button. You feel where it is bad, then you go back and change the building. That is the only fix this game believes in.
  8. Sign it off. Then the next room — and finished rooms stay open on the board to wander with nothing to do.

What this is built on

The people in it

Mara, Ollie and Jun are written characters, informed by the sources above. They are fiction clearly framed as fiction: nobody is quoted, nobody is a diagnosis, and none of their lines are testimony. The slot for real statements from autistic people, collected with a charity partner and credited, remains built and remains empty until they are real.

What the game does and does not do

It will not shorten anyone’s waiting list. What it does is put the person who arranges rooms inside a model where depth of attention is the strength, interruption is the cost, and the environment — never the person — is the thing that changes. It is also a game an autistic player can actually sit with: no timers, no fail state, nothing missable, no sound.
